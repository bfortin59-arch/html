Building a Fort on a Hillside in Winter

In the winter of 1861–62, Union commanders ordered the construction of a defensive fort above West Point to protect Louisville and the vital supply line running along the Ohio River. The result was Fort Duffield — the largest earthen fort in Kentucky.

It was built not by engineers or seasoned veterans, but by young soldiers facing cold, sickness, and exhaustion.

Why the Fort Was Built

  • Louisville was a major Union supply hub
  • Confederate forces threatened the region
  • West Point sat at a strategic crossroads
  • The high ridge offered a commanding view of the rivers

Union leadership needed a strong defensive position — and they needed it fast.

The Work Begins

Regiments assigned to West Point were ordered to construct the fort immediately. Most were new recruits with little training and no experience in large-scale engineering.

  • Cutting timber for walls and revetments
  • Digging trenches and shaping earthworks
  • Hauling dirt up steep slopes
  • Building cabins and shelters
  • Clearing the ridge for visibility

Hardship on the Hill

The winter of 1861–62 was bitterly cold. Soldiers lacked proper clothing, tools, and medical care. Many slept in makeshift shelters or under the open sky.

  • Pneumonia
  • Measles
  • Exposure
  • Exhaustion
  • Limited rations

A Fort Completed

By early 1862, Fort Duffield stood ready:

  • A large earthen perimeter
  • Angled walls for defense
  • Overlooks covering the rivers and roads
  • A protected interior for troops and supplies

Though the fort never saw battle, its presence helped secure the region during a critical moment in the war.

Their Work Still Stands

More than 160 years later, the earthworks carved by those young soldiers remain visible. Their labor, sacrifice, and resilience are preserved in the quiet curves of the fort’s walls.
