A Living History: From Civil War Earthwork to Community Landmark

Fort Duffield’s story didn’t end when the soldiers marched away. For more than 160 years, the fort has passed through periods of abandonment, rediscovery, restoration, and community stewardship.

1862–1900: After the War

  • The fort is largely abandoned after early-war threats shift south.
  • Earthworks remain intact due to the fort’s remote hillside location.
  • The cemetery remains known to the community, though markers deteriorate.

1900–1960: Quiet Decades

  • The fort sits mostly untouched.
  • Trees and undergrowth slowly reclaim the earthworks.
  • Occasional local efforts attempt to mark the cemetery, but no formal preservation exists.

1960–1990: Rediscovery & Early Preservation

  • Interest in Civil War history grows nationally.
  • Local historians begin documenting the fort’s layout and cemetery.
  • Community members push for recognition of the site.
  • Early volunteer groups begin clearing trails and marking features.

1990–2000: Formal Restoration Begins

  • The Fort Duffield Heritage Committee forms.
  • Major clearing and restoration work begins on the earthworks.
  • Trails are established for public access.
  • The cemetery receives renewed attention and respectful care.
  • Interpretive signs and basic visitor information are added.

2000–Present: A Community-Driven Historic Site

  • Volunteers expand trails and improve access.
  • Educational programs begin for school groups.
  • Public events and commemorations grow in popularity.
  • New research clarifies the fort’s construction and regimental history.
  • Updated signage and interpretive materials are developed.
  • Fort Duffield becomes a valued local landmark and model of grassroots preservation.

A Legacy Preserved by Volunteers

The fort stands today because people chose to protect it. Every cleared trail, every repaired sign, every school group welcomed — it all reflects the dedication of the community.
