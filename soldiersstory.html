The Human Side of Fort Duffield

Behind every shovel of earth and every timber cut on the hillside was a young soldier far from home. Most were new recruits — teenagers and young men who had never seen war, never camped in winter, and never imagined building a fort on a steep Kentucky ridge.

Who They Were

  • Many were 17–22 years old
  • Most had enlisted only weeks earlier
  • Many came from Indiana, Michigan, and Ohio
  • Few had military training
  • Nearly all faced illness during the winter of 1861–62

Life on the Ridge

Daily life at Fort Duffield was defined by work, cold, and sickness.

  • Cutting trees for fort walls
  • Digging trenches and shaping earthworks
  • Hauling dirt up steep slopes
  • Standing guard in freezing weather
  • Cooking simple meals over campfires
  • Caring for sick comrades

Shelter was limited. Many slept in makeshift huts or under canvas sheets that offered little protection from the wind.

Hardships and Illness

The winter was brutal. Exposure, pneumonia, and measles spread quickly through the regiments. Medical care was limited, and the climb up and down the ridge made everything harder.

  • Cold rain and snow
  • Thin blankets and inadequate clothing
  • Poor nutrition
  • Exhaustion from long workdays
  • Disease spreading through crowded camps

Letters Home

Surviving letters describe missing family, fear of illness, pride in their work, hope that the war would end quickly, and the beauty of the ridge despite the hardship.

A Legacy of Service

Though Fort Duffield never saw battle, the soldiers who built it played a crucial role in securing Louisville and the Union supply line. Their labor helped shape the early course of the Civil War in Kentucky.
